## Accent & variety plan (future recordings only)

Applies **only to recordings not yet produced**. All 24 listening masters (Units 01–10 and Module Reviews 1–2) are on disk and are not re-recorded. `u04-listening-02.mp3` was delivered with two female voices, shown to the learner as Speaker A / Speaker B.

**Status:** no recordings are pending. The rules below apply only if a listening is ever added or replaced.

**Casting and direction rules**
- Realise a variety through **casting or voice selection only**. Scripts stay verbatim in standard spelling: no phonetic respellings, no dialect grammar or slang added, no eye-dialect.
- Aim for a natural, educated, moderate accent of the variety — never a caricature, and never "strong accent" as a direction.
- Intelligibility first: pace and clarity as in the existing voice directions. If a take is hard to follow for a B2+/C1 listener, re-take it rather than simplifying the script.
- International / L2 speakers: highly proficient, fluent and fully intelligible; their accent is present but no errors are scripted in. Where the script makes the speaker Brazilian (u05 l1, r01 l1 · Marcus), cast a proficient Brazilian speaker of English.
- For TTS production, choose a voice whose native variety matches the plan; do not prompt a voice to imitate an accent it was not built for.
- Two-voice dialogues mixing varieties are intentional: the listener hears the contrast without anything in the script drawing attention to it.
- Record the variety used in the per-file **Accent / variety** line below when the master is delivered.

## Unit 02: The self you become in another language

### Listening Activity 1: A Room With Different Acoustics (l1)
- **File Destination**: `/audio/en/unit-02/u02-listening-01.mp3`
- **Format**: Mini-podcast
- **CEFR Level**: B2+ → C1
- **Target Duration**: ~133 seconds
- **Voice Direction**: One adult voice; reflective, natural conversational pace; clear international English
- **Status**: READY (Master on disk)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · distinguish what changed from what remained constant
  1. Second listen · identify why the speaker felt more direct in English
  1. Third listen · notice dependent prepositions and aspect markers

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
When people ask whether I am different in English, I usually hesitate. If I say yes, it sounds as though I have two separate personalities, which is obviously an exaggeration. But if I say no, I am ignoring a genuine experience. I have been living between Portuguese and English for over ten years now, and the two languages definitely bring out different sides of me. In Portuguese, I grew up learning that politeness means softness. If you want to contradict someone, you begin by validating their feelings, you make a joke, you apologise for disagreeing. In English, especially in academic and professional contexts, I learned that clarity is a form of respect. Saying “I don't think that conclusion follows from the evidence” is not considered an attack; it's considered an invitation to discuss. For a long time, I interpreted this as proof that English had made me a braver, more assertive person. But looking back, that claim confused an interpretation with a fact. The fact was that I had learned a specific set of phrases in English that made disagreement feel safe. Those phrases arrived without the emotional weight of childhood memories. I wasn't being braver; I was using a script that had lower emotional cost. My core values hadn't changed at all. What had changed was the linguistic affordance of the room I was standing in. When we switch languages, we don't switch souls. We switch repertoires, and we step into different social expectations.
```

### Listening Activity 2: Personality Shift or Social Feedback Loop? (l2)
- **File Destination**: `/audio/en/unit-02/u02-listening-02.mp3`
- **Format**: Discussion / Debate
- **CEFR Level**: C1
- **Target Duration**: ~120 seconds
- **Voice Direction**: Two adult voices (Speaker A and Speaker B); thoughtful, probing conversation
- **Status**: READY (Master on disk)
- **Accent / variety**: as recorded in the delivered master (not re-recorded)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · identify the disagreement about cause and effect
  1. Second listen · track Speaker B’s argument about the audience
  1. Third listen · notice hedging and qualifying markers

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
Speaker A: I still find the concept of bicultural frame switching convincing. When bilingual speakers switch from Spanish or Portuguese to English, their responses on personality questionnaires actually change. They score higher on assertiveness and extraversion. That suggests the language itself activates a distinct cultural framework.

Speaker B: But what are those questionnaires measuring? If I take a personality test in English, I am answering in a language I mostly use in professional or international environments. Those environments value directness and assertiveness. So is the language activating an innate English self, or am I simply recalling the contexts in which I use English?

Speaker A: Experiments control for that. Even when the setting is kept identical, showing participants cultural icons or changing the prompt language shifts their ratings.

Speaker B: It shifts their ratings, yes, but a shift in ratings isn't proof of an altered personality. There is a social feedback loop that often gets overlooked. When an immigrant speaks accented English, listeners react to that accent with specific expectations. The speaker then adapts to the listener's reaction. What looks like an internal switch caused by grammar or vocabulary might actually be a negotiation with the audience.

Speaker A: So you see language as a conduit rather than a cause?

Speaker B: Not just a conduit. Language provides the tools. But calling the result a “new personality” conflates the tool with the person using it. We have one expanding repertoire, not two incompatible identities.
```

## Unit 03: Memory is not a recording

### Listening Activity 1: The Photograph on the Mantelpiece (l1)
- **File Destination**: `/audio/en/unit-03/u03-listening-01.mp3`
- **Format**: Mini-podcast
- **CEFR Level**: B2+ → C1
- **Target Duration**: ~125 seconds
- **Voice Direction**: One adult voice; reflective, measured, analytical storytelling
- **Status**: READY (Master on disk)
- **Accent / variety**: as recorded in the delivered master (not re-recorded)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · identify the contradiction between the memory and the physical evidence
  1. Second listen · track how the family story contaminated the recollection
  1. Third listen · notice narrative tenses and verbs of memory

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
For nearly twenty years, I had a vivid memory of my grandfather's seventieth birthday party. I remembered standing on a wooden bench in his garden, wearing a yellow knitted jumper, singing a song while everyone clapped. The details were remarkably crisp: the smell of charcoal from the barbecue, the afternoon light cutting across the lawn, and my grandfather smiling with his hands in his pockets. It was one of the cornerstone memories of my childhood. Then, three years ago, my aunt brought out a photo album. There was the birthday party, there was the garden, and there was the bench. But standing on the bench was my younger cousin Lucas, wearing the yellow jumper. I was sitting at a folding table twenty feet away, eating a piece of cake. At first, I refused to believe the photograph. I thought it had to be a different year. But the date on the back was unmistakable. What had happened was a classic case of source confusion. Over the years, my family had repeatedly told the story of Lucas singing on the bench. Because I had heard the narrative so many times, my mind had reconstructed the scene from the inside, gradually placing myself at the centre of the action. The vividness of the sensory details—the light, the smell, the warmth—was completely genuine as a subjective experience, but it was not evidence of accuracy. Memory doesn't record footage and lock it in a vault. It retrieves fragments, fills the gaps with plausible narratives, and reassembles them every time we remember.
```

### Listening Activity 2: Flashbulb Memories and the Illusion of Permanence (l2)
- **File Destination**: `/audio/en/unit-03/u03-listening-02.mp3`
- **Format**: Academic discussion
- **CEFR Level**: C1
- **Target Duration**: ~115 seconds
- **Voice Direction**: Two adult voices (Dr. Rivera and Professor Evans); collegiate, brisk, analytical
- **Status**: READY (Master on disk)
- **Accent / variety**: as recorded in the delivered master (not re-recorded)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · define flashbulb memory and the core empirical finding
  1. Second listen · distinguish confidence ratings from factual accuracy scores
  1. Third listen · notice Past Perfect and hedging verbs

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
Dr. Rivera: In the aftermath of major historical catastrophes, people often swear they remember every single detail of where they were and what they were doing the moment they heard the news. We call these flashbulb memories. The common assumption is that intense emotional arousal acts like an indelible stamp, preserving the event forever.

Professor Evans: And yet, when researchers test those memories longitudinally—interviewing witnesses the day after the event and then re-interviewing them one, three, and ten years later—a completely different pattern emerges.

Dr. Rivera: Exactly. Neisser's famous study on the Challenger space shuttle disaster demonstrated this beautifully. On the morning after the explosion, participants wrote down detailed accounts. Three years later, when they described the event again, almost a third of them gave accounts that were wildly inconsistent with what they had originally recorded.

Professor Evans: But here is the fascinating part: their subjective confidence had not declined at all. They were just as certain of their revised memories as they were of their original ones. One participant even looked at her own handwriting from day one and said, “That's my handwriting, but I could swear that never happened.”

Dr. Rivera: Which brings us to the critical distinction: emotional intensity cements confidence, but it does not protect accuracy. When a narrative feels unshakeable, that certainty tells us about the speaker's current conviction, not the reliability of the historical record.
```

- **Target Language**: mistake emotional intensity for factual accuracy · reconstructed after the fact · a genuine subjective experience, but not proof · confidence does not guarantee
- **Grammar Focus**: Narrative tenses; Past Perfect for prior events; verbs of recollection (remember, remind, recall); modal concession (may feel, doesn’t necessarily mean)

## Unit 04: The stories we tell ourselves

  1. First listen · identify the original turning point story and its hidden assumption
  1. Second listen · notice what structural conditions had been omitted
  1. Third listen · track Past Perfect Continuous and gerund/infinitive contrasts

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
For years, my standard career narrative was built around a single dramatic turning point. I had been working at a corporate law firm in São Paulo, feeling increasingly alienated by the seventy-hour weeks and the endless commercial litigation. One rainy Tuesday, or so the story went, I walked into my partner's office, handed in my resignation without having another job lined up, and decided to dedicate my life to investigative journalism. I used to tell that story at dinner parties as an example of pure personal courage. I presented it as an act of solitary agency: a hero breaking free from corporate golden handcuffs. But about five years ago, I began re-examining the assumptions inside that self-narrative. What had I omitted? To begin with, I had been living rent-free in an apartment owned by my parents. I had accumulated substantial savings, and I had no dependants. Furthermore, a senior editor at a leading magazine had already told me informally that he would consider commissioning freelance pieces if I left the firm. My resignation hadn't been an act of reckless bravery; it had been a calculated transition made possible by an invisible safety net. When we tell stories about our lives, we naturally select moments that make us look principled and decisive. We tend to transform collective, privileged conditions into individual virtue. Acknowledging that context doesn't mean regretting the decision. It simply means admitting that our favourite stories about ourselves often depend on what we choose to leave out.
```

```text
Speaker B: We see it everywhere in memoirs and commencement speeches: “Losing that company was the best thing that ever happened to me.” It sounds inspirational, but it imposes a retrospective coherence that can be quite coercive.

Speaker B: And it distorts causal reality. Sometimes an enterprise fails because the market collapsed or someone committed fraud, not because the universe wanted to teach the founder humility. Insisting on finding meaning can prevent us from seeing the structural causes of an outcome.

Speaker A: So the challenge is allowing an event to remain unresolved—admitting that some experiences are simply destructive, without feeling compelled to package them as triumphs in disguise.
```

## Unit 05: Leaving changes you

- **Target Duration**: ~130 seconds

- **Accent / variety**: as recorded in the delivered master (not re-recorded)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · identify why returning to a former home felt disorienting
  1. Second listen · track the baseline error (what would have changed anyway)
  1. Third listen · notice used to, would, and be used to

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
When I left Curitiba to work in London, I used to imagine that my city would freeze in time until I got back. We all fall into that trap. We imagine home as a museum of our own childhood, waiting patiently for our return. When I finally moved back after four years, I was going to resume my old life as though nothing of consequence had happened. But almost immediately, I felt like a stranger in my own neighbourhood. The local bakery where my friends and I would sit on Saturday mornings had been demolished and replaced by a pharmacy. My closest friends were busy with mortgages and small children; they were no longer used to meeting spontaneously after work. At first, I felt a deep sense of betrayal. I thought: “London changed me so much that I no longer belong here.” But that conclusion was based on an invisible baseline error. I was comparing my present self to a Curitiba that no longer existed. The city hadn't stayed still, and my friends hadn't stayed still. Even if I had never left Brazil, I wouldn't have continued sitting in that bakery forever. People age, friendships evolve, and cities rebuild themselves. Leaving certainly altered my habits—I was now used to walking everywhere and eating dinner at six in the evening. But attributing the entire sense of estrangement to migration was an exaggeration. What I was actually mourning was not the loss of a city, but the passage of four years of ordinary time.
```

## Unit 06: What makes love feel real?

### Listening Activity 1: The Confusion Between Intensity and Care (l1)
- **File Destination**: `/audio/en/unit-06/u06-listening-01.mp3`
- **Format**: Mini-podcast
- **CEFR Level**: B2+ → C1
- **Target Duration**: ~125 seconds
- **Voice Direction**: One adult voice; analytical, calm, warm, reflective
- **Status**: READY (Master on disk)
- **Accent / variety**: as recorded in the delivered master (not re-recorded)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · identify the difference between emotional intensity and relational care
  1. Second listen · analyze why passion is neither necessary nor sufficient for enduring love
  1. Third listen · notice modals of necessity, possibility, and deduction

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
When we are young, culture teaches us that emotional intensity is the single most reliable proof of love. If a relationship makes your heart race, if you feel anxious when they don't reply, if the world seems grey whenever they leave the room, you conclude that the bond must be extraordinarily real. But over time, one learns that intensity is often evidence of insecurity rather than affection. Rapid dopamine spikes and obsessive preoccupation indicate that an attachment system has been activated under conditions of uncertainty. They do not demonstrate that you actually know, respect, or care for the person in front of you. To use the philosopher's terms: intense feeling may be a common accompanying feature of early romance, but it is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for love. It isn't sufficient because someone can experience overwhelming passion while treating their partner with profound cruelty or indifference. And it isn't necessary because long-standing partners often experience periods where passionate intensity subsides, yet their commitment, mutual attentiveness, and daily care remain rock-solid. When we confuse intensity with love, we risk dismissing stable affection as boring, while mistaking turbulent drama for depth. Real care is evidenced by how two people treat each other on an ordinary, unglamorous Tuesday morning when there is no audience and no adrenaline.
```

```text
Julian: That's a fair challenge. What about responsiveness? What if love requires being attentive to the other person's reality and revising your assumptions about them over time?

Elena: That feels much closer to a defensible necessary condition. ## Unit 07: When friendship stops being simple
```

### Listening Activity 1: The Unanswered Message (l1)
- **File Destination**: `/audio/en/unit-07/u07-listening-01.mp3`
- **Format**: Mini-podcast
- **CEFR Level**: B2+ → C1
- **Target Duration**: ~125 seconds
- **Voice Direction**: One adult voice; thoughtful, vulnerable yet analytically disciplined
- **Status**: READY (Master on disk)
- **Accent / variety**: as recorded in the delivered master (not re-recorded)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · map the progression from simple possibility to mounting probability
  1. Second listen · distinguish between legitimate silence and costly ambiguity
  1. Third listen · notice modal perfects and degrees of deduction

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
When a close friend leaves a message unanswered for three days, you don't immediately assume the friendship is collapsing. In the realm of pure possibility, dozens of benign explanations exist: she could have lost her phone, her mother might have fallen ill, or work might have overwhelmed her schedule. At first, those possibilities hold equal weight. But when the unanswered message is followed by two cancellations, a sudden change in tone, and an awkward excuse at a mutual acquaintance's birthday dinner, the calculus shifts from possibility to probability. We are no longer dealing with a single isolated event; we are observing a pattern. In my own twenties, I made the mistake of confusing possibility with equal likelihood. Whenever a friend began pulling away, I would invent elaborate, generous hypotheses to avoid facing the most probable conclusion: that her priorities had changed, and that I was no longer in her inner circle. By clinging to far-fetched possibilities, I spared myself temporary discomfort, but I ended up carrying all the relational labour. Ambiguity can be a gentle buffer when feelings are still taking shape, but when one person uses silence to manage their boundaries while the other person absorbs all the uncertainty, ambiguity stops being kind. It becomes an avoidance strategy. Learning to think clearly about friendship didn't make me cynical; it simply taught me to separate what could theoretically be true from what the evidence actually supports.
```

## Unit 08: When love has to be explained

### Listening Activity 1: The Footnote on the Invitation (l1)
- **File Destination**: `/audio/en/unit-08/u08-listening-01.mp3`
- **Format**: Mini-podcast
- **CEFR Level**: B2+ → C1
- **Target Duration**: ~130 seconds
- **Voice Direction**: One adult voice; articulate, reflective, warm, subtle social irony
- **Status**: READY (Master on disk)
- **Accent / variety**: as recorded in the delivered master (not re-recorded)
- **Listening Passes**:
  1. First listen · identify the social script that demands an explanation
  1. Second listen · track the distinction between receiving a plot versus receiving a prompt
  1. Third listen · observe relative clauses and participle clauses

**Verbatim Script**:
```text
When a heterosexual couple announces their engagement, nobody asks them to define the nature of their relationship. The cultural script arrives pre-printed. Everyone knows what an engagement party signifies, what kind of gifts to buy, and what seating arrangements are expected at dinner. The couple receives what sociologists call a plot: a narrative that moves forward smoothly without requiring any explanatory footnotes. But when two people form a partnership that falls outside the dominant heterosexual or monogamous scripts—whether as long-term queer companions, co-parents living in non-romantic partnerships, or polyamorous families—their bond is immediately met with a question mark.
```
