### Unit 11: Who Gets Heard in a Democracy?
- **File 1:** `audio/unit-11-listening-1.mp3`
  - **Title:** Dialogue: Representation and Disillusionment
  - **Voices:** Dr. Arthur Vance & Elena Ward (Political Scientists)
  - **Script:**
    `[Vance]: Elena, when citizens say 'my vote doesn't count,' they are rarely expressing complete apathy. More often, they are observing an empirical reality: that formal voting rights do not translate into substantive policy influence when political parties are captured by corporate finance.`
    `[Ward]: Exactly, Arthur. Political scientists call this the 'representation deficit.' In many modern democracies, the public is permitted to vote once every four years, but the daily agenda of parliament is set by well-funded lobbying coalitions. The procedural right to speak exists; the institutional capacity to be heard has been hollowed out.`
    `[Vance]: Which creates a dangerous legitimacy crisis. When voters realize that shifting governments produces zero change in their economic security, they become vulnerable to populist demagogues who promise to smash the entire system.`
- **File 2:** `audio/unit-11-listening-2.mp3`
  - **Title:** Monologue: The Architecture of Democratic Voice
  - **Voice:** Julian Stern (Philosopher)
  - **Script:**
    `Democratic participation cannot be reduced to a mechanical ritual performed at a ballot box. A genuine democracy is a continuous conversation among equals. When a society allows economic inequality to dictate who possesses the megaphone, it ceases to function as a republic of citizens and degenerates into an oligarchy of capital. To restore democratic faith, we must build institutions where the quietest voices carry as much structural weight as the loudest wealth.`

### Unit 13: Left, Right and Everything In Between
- **File 1:** `audio/unit-13-listening-1.mp3`
  - **Title:** Dialogue: Beyond the One-Dimensional Spectrum
  - **Voices:** Dr. Arthur Vance & Elena Ward (Political Theorists)
  - **Script:**
    `[Vance]: Elena, the traditional one-dimensional line from left to right was born in the French National Assembly of 1789, where supporters of the king sat on the right and revolutionaries sat on the left. But can that eighteenth-century geometry describe modern political beliefs?`
    `[Ward]: It fails completely, Arthur. A citizen might favor progressive environmental regulation while holding traditional religious social values; another might support aggressive free markets while opposing foreign military interventions. The two-axis Nolan chart—separating economic freedom from personal liberty—is far more accurate.`
    `[Vance]: And when we reduce human worldviews to a single binary axis, we encourage tribal polarization: voters begin defending policies they disagree with simply because their 'side' endorsed them.`

  - **Voice:** Julian Stern (Philosopher)

If you cannot explain why an intelligent, compassionate person would vote for your political rival, you do not understand the political terrain. To understand the right, you must understand their reverence for historical continuity and personal responsibility; to understand the left, you must understand their hunger for structural justice and egalitarian solidarity. True wisdom begins when you recognize that civilization requires both the engine of reform and the brakes of preservation.`

A child born to affluent parents inherits private tutoring, stable housing, and influential social networks; a child born in poverty inherits toxic stress and underfunded schools. Until we decouple human dignity from economic market value, the promise of equal opportunity remains a cruel fiction.`

### Unit 20: The Cost of Convenience
- **File 1:** `audio/unit-20-listening-1.mp3`

  - **Voices:** Julian Stern & Elena Ward (Philosophers of Culture)
  - **Script:**

    `[Ward]: While a central heating thermostat is a device: it delivers the commodity of warmth instantaneously without requiring any physical engagement or skill.`
    `[Stern]: And modern convenience platforms—from food delivery apps to streaming video—convert every meaningful focal practice into a disembodied, frictionless commodity. We gain speed, but we lose connection to the physical world.`
- **File 2:** `audio/unit-20-listening-2.mp3`
  - **Title:** Monologue: The Dignity of Difficulty
  - **Voice:** Sofia Alvarez (Essayist)
  - **Script:**
    `We have engineered our world to eliminate waiting, eliminate effort, and eliminate friction. But human character is forged in friction. When you knead bread with your hands, when you play a musical instrument, when you write an essay on a blank page, the difficulty is not an obstacle to be eliminated—it is the very engine of self-discovery. A life without friction is a life without weight.`

## 7. Recording Verification & Quality Checklist
Before publishing generated MP3 files into the `public/audio/` directories, ensure:
1. **Pacing:** Normal human conversational and lecture speed (~140–160 words per minute for dialogue, ~120–140 wpm for philosophical monologues).
2. **Pauses:** Respect all em-dashes (0.3s breath), colons (0.5s hold), and paragraph breaks (0.8s silence).
3. **Pronunciation Fidelity:** Check British/US phonetic consistency for technical terms (*punctum*, *epistemic*, *sovereignty*, *anaphora*).
4. **Volume & Leveling:** Normalize output audio files to -16 LUFS with -1.0 dB true peak.
